What's this?

I made this tool primarily for my own amusement. I prefer to size my images and videos such that both dimensions are divisible by 16 or 8 because i know most compression algorithms (like JPEG) work in blocks of 8×8 pixels. The "FRAME" element above is supposed to represent your TV/Monitor but could just as easily be interpreted as a physical picture frame. The "CONTENT" box inside of the "FRAME" is your image or video content.

Want to get the aspect ratio of an image? drag and drop it into the blue content box :D

The idea of matching any particular film format/size to a megapixel count sounds simple on the surface but is pretty complex in reality. In my case, with my photo lab film and digital media background, I decided to go for a 24 megapixel camera that could take legacy known good lenses once they were just within my budget (around 2014-2015) because the sample images I was seeing from dpreview.com were finally matching or exceeding the detail I knew was possible from high quality (but relatively affordable) scans of low ISO 35 mm photo and slide film. If you want some more scientific and highly technical details on this subject ClarkVision.com has them. After getting a 24 MP Sony camera I was extremely happy with the amount of detail I was able to get by "scanning" 35 mm and larger format still photo films with my low-budget setup.

The claims of an IMAX movie being 12k, 16k, or 18k kind of inspired me to go through everything I know and make the tables above. Full HD = 1920×1080 = 1080p = 1k = 2 megapixels. I've always hated the "4k" type nomenclature as it's a fuzzy number that probably came from a marketing exec that, at best, didn't know or care much or about photography. 4k only refers to the width of the image frame being *_approximately_* 4000 pixels. The actual frame size could be 4096×2160 or 3840×2160 or 3840×1644, which works out to 8.8 mp, 8.2 mp, and 6.3 mp, respectively. One of the reasons I was given for "why it's called 4k" years ago is that it's 4x a 1080p frame, which is true for 3840×2160 4K UHD frame sizes and display resolutions but not quite for DCI 4K or any other pixel count that might get the "4k" label slapped onto it by unscrupoulus businessmen.

But we're talking about an IMAX movie shot on 70 mm (actually 65 mm) film and film doesn't have pixels! Film does have grain but it's not correct to state that all 70 mm (or 65 mm) film has 12, 16, or 18 million grains. The camera lens, film ISO speed (ISO 50 will have less visible grain than ISO 400) and the film developing processes (chemistry, timing, printing to workprint and final prints) will have an impact of the number of percievable grains any particular film might have.

So, using the details I've collected above, we'll go with an IMAX 15/70 movie having a film frame size of 70×48.5 mm and an area of 3395 square mm. Standard 135 photo film frames are 24×36 mm (864 square mm) and ISO 100 film could have 6 megapixels of detail, wich is just a little below 4K definitions of approximately 8 megapixels. However, 35 mm photo film frames are bigger than 35 mm movie frames at 16×22 mm and 352 square mm. So IMAX movies could have about 9 to 10x more detail than 35 mm movies - 3395 mm is 9.6 times more than 352 square mm.

Ok, 35 mm photo film = 864 mm square = 6 megapixels and is 2.45 times larger than a 35 mm movie film frame. 6 mp / 2.45 = 2.44 megapixels for 35 mm movies. What was the megapixel count for 1080p again? 2073600 pixels = 2 megapixels. So in theory, if an IMAX 15/70 film has 10x the detail of a 35 mm movie, that puts us over 20 megapixels for IMAX. Theoretically. Reality is almost always different than initial theories.

We still need the kays, boss. How many kays in IMAX 15/70?

1920×1080 = 2.07 megapixels = 1K because 4K is 2x 1920 no matter how much I dislike it. A 35 mm movie frame = 2.44 megapixels = 1.17 times larger than 1080p. 1K (1080p) * 1.17 = 1.17K for a 35 mm movie film frame. IMAX 15/70 is 10x that so 11.7K, this is probably where the 12K number for IMAX movies comes from.

The IMAX 15/70 film frame has an aspect ratio of 1.43. If we 10x the 1920 from 1080p (1K) that gives us a frame width of 19200 and the 1.43 (or 0.692) aspect ratio gives us a height of 13286, which is problematic if it's not divisible by a multiple of 8. 64 * 208 = 13312. So our theoretical IMAX 15/70 film frame would have a digital dimension of 19200×13312 and an area of 255,590,400 pixels or 256 megapixels, which isn't the point, but maybe worth noting. The 19200 width (from 10xing 1080p's width) would probably be classified as 20K, which helps set a probably not-insane upper limit for claims from studios or other enthusiasts, theoretically, of course.

I'm in no way insinuating that IMAX film has anything like 200+ megapixels of resolution! Refer back to the page on ClarkVision.com and you'll find 78 MPix noted for the 56×70mm frame of Fuji Velvia ISO 50 120 (65 mm) medium format photo film and 240 MPix for 4×5 inch large format film. The IMAX 15/70 frame size/area is very similar at 48.5×70 mm so the actual megapixels of detail you could get from digitizing IMAX movies is extremely likely to be closer to 78 than 240 or 256!

The most commonly discussed 16K resolution as of 2025, is 15360×8640, which doubles the pixel count of 8K UHD in each dimension, for a total of four times as many pixels or 132.7 megapixels. The theoretical 20K we derived from IMAX 15/70 film above nearly doubles that. Will TPTB double 16K to 32K when the need arises for something more than that? Will they compromise and go with 24K? 20 isn't a number divisible by 8 so when it comes to digital things it's less than ideal for the numerical jumps or steps seen in other things, like RAM or hard disks, which is the only reason I have for not liking "20K" as a number/term at first glance.

Just a note that fits here even though it's about photo film and not movie film: High-quality 35mm film under ideal conditions can resolve 80–100 line pairs per millimeter, which translates to roughly 20–25 megapixels of actual image information when scanned at high resolution, though most real-world images contain 10–15 megapixels of usable detail due to grain, diffraction, motion blur, and lens limitations (KenRockwell.com).

In the end, if people are debating how many pixels or kays there are in any given movie I think maybe they've lost the plot a bit, but it is interesting to someone like me with a non-scientific, more of a professional/amatuer enthusiast background in this stuff. I can even imagine being that marketing exec who might have grown tired of the incessant nitpicking from various sources and said "STOP IT! We're gonna call it 4K!" I don't understand why they went with the width of the frame, rather than height to arrive at the 4K classification rather than continuing to use the hight as they did with 1080p, 1080i, 720p, etc. Perhaps they were trying to cut their consumption costs on printer ink and 4K was cheaper to print than 2160p.
